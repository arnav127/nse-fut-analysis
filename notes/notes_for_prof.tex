\section*{1. Why we see what we see}
For each hypothesis: what we find, how we explain it, what supports that, and what is still a
guess. Sections 2 to 4 have the new tests behind these.

\subsection*{BH1: stock settlement prices are not pushed, the index is}
\textbf{Finding.} The stock settlement VWAP is no further from the 15:00 price on expiry days.
The Nifty 50, however, falls 6.2 bps relative to the Nifty Next 50 over the window on index
expiries ($p=0.008$), on weekly and monthly expiries alike.

\textbf{Explanation: it depends on how the contract settles.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Stock futures and options settle by \emph{delivery}. Someone long a future pays the
  agreed futures price for the shares whatever the settlement price is: a higher settlement
  price raises the final margin payment received but raises the price paid for the shares by
  the same amount. So there is nothing to gain from moving it.
  \item Evidence: in the last minutes of the window the expiring future moves one for one with
  the live cash price (slope 0.85, se 0.04), not with the VWAP that is already almost fixed. The market
  prices the future as a claim on shares, exactly as delivery implies.
  \item Options do have an incentive (the settlement price decides which strikes are in the
  money), but we find no pull of settlement prices towards strikes. Our guess: open interest
  near the money in a single stock is too small to justify the cost of moving its price for
  thirty minutes.
  \item The index settles in \emph{cash} against the closing value, so here moving the price
  does pay, and this is the one place we see a systematic move.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Why does the index fall? Two hypotheses (we cannot yet tell them apart):}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Unwinding of hedges.} Traders who are short index options or futures hedge with
  the constituent stocks. A cash-futures arbitrageur typically holds the stocks and is short
  the future (the future usually trades above cash). The cheapest way to exit is to sell the
  stocks in the settlement window, at the same average the derivative settles at. That is
  selling pressure concentrated in Nifty 50 stocks and absent in Next 50 stocks.
  \item \emph{Deliberate pressure.} Large holders of index options benefit from a lower
  settlement value and push it down.
\end{enumerate}
Both predict more aggressive selling in Nifty 50 constituents in the window on index expiries.
Hypothesis 1 predicts it mainly from proprietary and arbitrage accounts, roughly in proportion to
open interest, and a recovery the next morning. We see a recovery of about the same size the
next morning, but it is imprecise. Next step: aggressor flow by participant type in Nifty 50
stocks on index expiries.

\subsection*{BH2: the window is costlier in F\&O stocks}
\textbf{Finding.} In the window, spreads are about 10\% wider and depth 5 to 7\% lower on
expiry days, only in F\&O stocks.

\textbf{Explanation: volatility.} Prices in the window move about 45\% more on expiry days in F\&O
stocks and hardly more in placebo stocks. Market makers widen and thin their quotes when prices
move more, because they risk being stuck with inventory that moves against them and are more
likely to trade with someone better informed. Stocks whose volatility rose more had their spreads
widen more, and volatility accounts for about 70\% of the widening and all of the fall in depth.

\textbf{Why is the window more volatile? Our hypotheses:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item More volume is pulled into the window on expiry days (window share of volume up 2.4 pp
  relative to placebo): positions being closed, rolled or benchmarked to the close.
  \item \emph{Option hedging.} On expiry day options close to the money have very high gamma: a
  small price move swings their delta from 0 to 1. Dealers who hedge those options in the cash
  market have to trade a lot in the last half hour, and their trades add to price moves.
\end{itemize}
The remaining 3\% widening that volatility does not explain may come from market makers who are
active in both the cash market and the expiring options and are busy managing the options book
in the last half hour.

\subsection*{BH3: no one-sided pressure}
\textbf{Finding.} Order imbalance over the window is not more one-sided on expiry days, in cash or
in the expiring futures. It is more \emph{persistent} from one 5-minute block to the next.

\textbf{Explanation.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item No one gains from pushing stock prices (BH1), so the extra trading at the close is
  closing and benchmarking of positions, which comes from both buyers and sellers.
  \item Most futures positions are rolled \emph{before} expiry day: the next-month contract
  already has 52\% of futures volume the day before and 59\% on expiry day. Little is left to be
  unwound at the settlement price.
  \item The higher persistence is what large orders worked through the window look like:
  institutions executing against the closing price slice their orders evenly over the thirty
  minutes. Custodian (institutional) participation in window volume rises from 59\% to 65\%.
  Each such order is one-sided, but across stocks and institutions buys and sells offset.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{BH4: more fleeting orders}
\textbf{Finding.} The share of orders cancelled within a second rises (28.6\% to 30.1\%), only in
F\&O stocks.

\textbf{Explanation.} Algorithmic market makers re-price their quotes every time the price moves.
When prices move more often, more quotes are cancelled and replaced within a second. Once we
control for each stock's rise in volatility, the increase is small and not significant.
Entries rise as fast as cancellations, so this is more active quoting, not orders placed to
mislead and then pulled.

Checks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item drop stock-days with signs of news next morning (volume $>2\times$ median, or a very

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{overnight_by_expiry.pdf}\\
\small Each monthly expiry (orange) vs.\ ordinary sessions (grey).
\end{center}

\textbf{Takeaway:} mostly information arriving after the close. It comes from a few expiries,
is just as strong in placebo stocks (nothing settles there), and is small and insignificant
after the two adjustments.

\textbf{How news produces it.} News that nobody expected should be unrelated to the direction of
the window move, so on average it should not undo it. But with only 12 expiries a few big days
matter. On 24 Feb the market crashed during the day and bounced the next morning; high-beta stocks
fell most in the window and rose most the next morning, which looks like a reversal stock by
stock. Adjusting for beta removes about half of the effect for exactly this reason.

\textbf{What might be left.} The move from the morning to 15:00 is reversed much less than the
window move ($-0.05$ vs.\ $-0.26$), so perhaps not all of it is news. One hypothesis: the last
half hour of an expiry day (which is also close to month end) carries large closing orders from
funds, and market makers who absorb them at the close are paid by a small reversal the next
morning. That would show in all stocks, including placebo, as we find. Company announcement data
would let us separate the two.

\section*{4. Futures}
Rebuilt the order book of the expiring stock futures from the F\&O order files: all 12
expiries and the day before each, 84 to 100 stocks per day (a few stocks left F\&O during the
year, and one file part for 28 Apr is an incomplete download).

\toprule
Window, expiry vs.\ day before & Spread & Depth & Volatility \\
\midrule
Cash (same stocks) & $+10.0$\% & $-6.0$\% & $+45.8$\% \\
Expiring future & $+33.4$\% & $-6.4$\% & $+58.7$\% \\
\bottomrule

\begin{itemize}
  \item The futures spread jumps in the window on expiry day, about three times as much as in
  cash. Volatility explains only about a third of it (vs.\ 70\% in cash), and the fall in
  depth is significant even after controlling for volatility.
  \item Likely reason: it is the contract's last half hour. A market maker in the expiring future
  normally ends the day with a small long or short position and offsets it the next morning. On
  expiry day there is no next morning: any position left at 15:30 has to be settled by
  delivering or taking delivery of the shares, which needs the shares or the cash and carries
  extra margin. So market makers quote less and wider in the last minutes, and the few traders
  still in the expiring contract pay for it.
  \item Most positions have already rolled to next month (52\% of futures volume the day before,
  59\% on expiry day), so the traders left in the expiring contract are mostly those who missed
  the roll or want delivery. Liquidity providers follow the volume to the next contract.
  \item Still no one-sided pressure: futures $|$OIB$|$ in the window unchanged (0.18 vs.\ 0.18).
\end{itemize}
