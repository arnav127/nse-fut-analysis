\section{Hypotheses}\label{sec:hypotheses}

The study set out with four objectives: to compare the trajectory of the cash VWAP with the
traded price of the stock future in the settlement window; to identify which participants drive
expiry-day volume; to measure the role of algorithmic trading; and to detect abnormal order book
pressure, such as bursts of cancellations or aggressive immediate-or-cancel orders, ahead of the
settlement. The original design expressed these as thirty hypotheses. Testing each separately
makes the overall conclusion depend on how many happen to be significant, and the design is
consolidated here into four broad hypotheses. Every original hypothesis is retained as a
supporting measure of one of them, and one measure of each is designated the primary measure.

\paragraph{BH1: The settlement price behaves differently on expiry days.} Does the settlement
VWAP land somewhere unusual? Primary measure: the absolute distance between the settlement VWAP
and the mid at the start of the window. Supporting measures: the distance from the closing price, the final
minute's divergence, volume at the running VWAP, the timing of volume within the window, the
next-morning reversal of the window's move, the pricing of the expiring stock future, the index
settlement and the attraction of settlement prices to option strikes (Section~\ref{sec:bh1}).

\paragraph{BH2: Trading in the window is more costly.} Is the window thinner? Primary measure:
the quoted spread. Supporting measures: depth near the touch, displayed notional, the cost of
sweeping the book, price impact per unit of flow and per trade, realised variance, illiquidity
relative to the rest of the session and the recovery of the spread after shocks
(Section~\ref{sec:bh2}).

\paragraph{BH3: Trading in the window is more one-sided.} Is someone pushing the price? Primary
measure: the absolute order imbalance
\begin{equation*}
  |\mathrm{OIB}| = \left|\frac{BI - SI}{BI + SI}\right|,
\end{equation*}
where $BI$ and $SI$ are buyer- and seller-initiated volume. A balanced market has an imbalance
near zero; one-sided pressure keeps it away from zero. Because buying pressure on some days and
selling pressure on others would cancel in a signed average, the absolute value is tested.
Supporting measures: the persistence of the imbalance through the window, the variance ratio,
the persistence of signed order flow, the permanent share of price impact, the one-sidedness of
submitted orders and of the book, and the price effect of each participant category's flow
(Section~\ref{sec:bh3}).

\paragraph{BH4: Order behaviour and participation change.} How do participants trade? Primary
measure: the share of orders cancelled within one second of entry. Supporting measures, in four
groups: cancellation (cancellations per entry and in total), concealment (the share of resting
and submitted size that is concealed, the size of concealed orders and their replenishment),
urgency (the use of immediate-or-cancel orders) and participation (proprietary and algorithmic
volume and the share of each participant category in the window) (Section~\ref{sec:bh4}).

\subsection{Testing}

Each measure is computed per security and session and compared between each expiry session and
the control session of the same month, one pair per security and month, by a paired $t$-test.
The four primary tests are the basis of the conclusions, and the false discovery rate is
controlled across these four rather than across every measure. Each primary measure is also
compared between the securities with derivatives and the placebo group, which separates an
effect of the settlement from an effect of the calendar. Table~\ref{tab:primary} reports the
four primary tests; the full set of measures, grouped by broad hypothesis, is in
Appendix~\ref{app:tests}.

Two of the four primary tests are rejected. Trading in the window is more costly on expiry days
(BH2) and more of the orders submitted are withdrawn within a second (BH4), and in both cases the
change is specific to securities with derivatives. The settlement VWAP does not move further from
the price at the start of the window (BH1), and trading in the window is not more one-sided
(BH3). The sections that follow take each hypothesis in turn with its supporting measures.
