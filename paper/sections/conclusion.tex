\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Stock derivatives on the National Stock Exchange of India settle at the volume-weighted
average price of the last half hour of the expiry session, and it is often argued that this
arrangement invites participants with expiring positions to move the price in that window.
This paper examines the window with order-level data, over every session of 2022, against two
calendar controls and a placebo group of securities without derivatives.

Expiry changes how much is traded in the window. On monthly expiries a larger share of the
day's volume is traded in the settlement window, only in securities with derivatives and not
on weekly index expiries. The settlement price also moves further over the window and is
partly reversed the next morning, which is the pattern associated with price manipulation at
the close. But the reversal is of the same size in securities on which nothing settles, so it
is a property of the expiry session rather than of the settlement. No category of participant
trades in the window on expiry days in a way that moves the price more or is reversed more
than on other days. On the order book sample, the window is more expensive to trade in on
expiry days, but the measures that distinguish directional pressure from heavier two-sided
trading show no sign of a sustained push.

The derivatives explain why the incentive is weaker than it appears. Because stock futures
were settled by delivery, the settlement price changes only how the payment for the delivered
shares is split between the final margin and the delivery price. The expiring future is priced
accordingly: until the last minutes of the window it moves with the cash price rather than
with the nearly determined settlement price. Where the settlement price does matter, in
deciding which stock options are exercised, it shows no attraction to strikes. The additional
activity in the window is therefore better understood as the unwinding and hedging of
expiring positions than as an attempt to move the price at which they settle.

The analysis has limitations. The derivatives files cover four of the twelve expiries, so the
futures and option results rest on a smaller sample. Open interest is not observed, so
positions are inferred from traded volume, and the incentive cannot be measured security by
security. The categories in the order file distinguish institutional, proprietary and other
clients but not individual participants, so a strategy spread across participants or across
securities is not detected as such. The sample year is a single year, with Thursday expiries
and settlement by delivery; the incentive would be different, and larger, for contracts
settled in cash against the average, which include the index derivatives whose settlement
depends on the same window. Finally, the absence of a settlement-specific effect in the
average does not exclude manipulation in individual cases, which requires surveillance data
that identifies the trader.
