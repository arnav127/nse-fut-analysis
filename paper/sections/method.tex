\section{Measures and estimation}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The settlement price and its displacement}

For security $i$ on session $t$ let $S_{it}$ be the settlement price, the volume-weighted
average price of the window, and $P^{\mathrm{pre}}_{it}$ the volume-weighted average price of
the ten minutes before the window opens. The move over the window is
\begin{equation}
  D_{it} = 10^{4}\,\ln\bigl(S_{it} / P^{\mathrm{pre}}_{it}\bigr),
\end{equation}
in basis points. Let $O_{i,t+1}$ be the volume-weighted average price of the first thirty
minutes of the following session. The next-morning return from the settlement price is
\begin{equation}
  R_{i,t+1} = 10^{4}\,\ln\bigl(O_{i,t+1} / S_{it}\bigr).
\end{equation}
A settlement price displaced by trading that does not reflect information is followed by a
return in the opposite direction once that trading stops, so that $R_{i,t+1}$ is negatively
related to $D_{it}$. Some reversal is expected on any session, since part of the price
movement caused by liquidity demand is temporary. The test is whether the reversal is larger
on monthly expiries:
\begin{equation}
  R_{i,t+1} = \alpha_i + \beta D_{it}
      + \sum_{k} \gamma_k \, D_{it} \, \mathbb{1}[t \in k]
      + \sum_{k} \delta_k \, \mathbb{1}[t \in k] + \varepsilon_{it},
  \label{eq:reversal}
\end{equation}
where $k$ ranges over monthly expiries, weekly index expiries and month ends, and
$\alpha_i$ is a security fixed effect. The coefficient $\beta$ measures the reversal on
ordinary sessions and $\gamma_{\text{monthly}}$ the additional reversal on monthly expiries.
This is the signature used in studies of closing price manipulation
\citep{carhart2002leaning, hillion2004price, comerton2011measuring}. A value of
$\gamma_{\text{monthly}}$ that is negative in the derivatives groups and zero in the placebo
group would indicate that the settlement price is displaced on expiry days by trading
related to the settlement.

\subsection{Window activity}

The abnormal activity in the window is measured by three quantities: the share of the
session's volume traded in the window, the absolute move $|D_{it}|$, and the absolute
distance between the volume-weighted average price of the final five minutes and the
settlement price. Each is regressed on indicators for the three session types with security
fixed effects, so that the coefficients measure the difference from ordinary sessions of the
same security.

\subsection{Order flow and its price effect}

Every trade has an aggressor, the side whose order arrived later and crossed the spread;
order numbers are assigned in order of arrival, so the aggressor is the side with the larger
order number. For each security and session, net aggressor flow in the window is the volume
bought by aggressive buyers minus the volume sold by aggressive sellers, expressed as a
percentage of the security's median session volume. It is computed in total and separately
by the category of the aggressor. The window move and the next-morning return are regressed
on this flow and its interaction with the session types. The first regression measures how
far a given flow moves the settlement price; the second how much of that movement is
subsequently reversed.

\subsection{Inference}

Observations are correlated across securities within a session, and on an expiry day this
correlation is the rule, not the exception. Standard errors are therefore clustered by
session. Because the monthly expiry coefficients are identified from only
\TapeNMonthlyexpiry{} sessions, the main coefficients are also compared with their
randomization distribution. The same regression is estimated \RiDraws{} times with the
monthly expiries removed and replaced by one ordinary session drawn at random from each
month. The randomization $p$-value is the share of these draws in which the coefficient is
at least as large in absolute value as the one observed. It does not rely on the asymptotic
approximation that a small number of clusters makes unreliable.

\subsection{Futures convergence}

On an ordinary session a stock future trades at the cash price plus the cost of carry. On the
expiry day it settles at the average cash price of the window, part of which is already
determined during the window. If it is priced as such, its price at minute $m$ should be close
to the projected settlement price
\begin{equation}
  \hat S_{m} = \frac{V_m \bar{S}_m + (V - V_m)\, s_m}{V},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{S}_m$ is the average price of the window up to minute $m$, $V_m$ the volume up to
that minute, $V$ the volume of the whole window and $s_m$ the current cash price. The basis
$\ln(F_m/s_m)$ is regressed on $\ln(\hat S_m / s_m)$ with a fixed effect for each security
and session, which absorbs the cost of carry. A slope of one indicates that the future is
priced as the average to which it settles; a slope of zero that it follows the cash price.
The regression uses the realised window volume, which is not known during the window, and is
a description of pricing rather than a trading rule.

\subsection{Attraction to option strikes}

Let $K$ be the set of stock option strikes of security $i$ traded in the expiry month and
$\Delta_i$ the local strike interval. The distance of the settlement price to a strike is
\begin{equation}
  d_{it} = \min_{k \in K} \bigl|S_{it} - k\bigr| / \Delta_i \;\in\; [0, \tfrac{1}{2}],
\end{equation}
which has a mean of one quarter if the settlement price is unrelated to the strikes. It is
compared between the expiry day and the other sessions of the same expiry month, with a
fixed effect for each security and month, and within the expiry day between the settlement
price and the pre-window price. Delta hedging by option writers and trading by option holders
both predict that the settlement price is drawn toward strikes on the expiry day
\citep{ni2005stock}.
