\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Derivatives that settle against a price formed in the underlying market give their holders an
interest in that price. When the settlement price is a single closing print, a participant
with a large expiring position can move it at little cost, and the resulting distortions are
well documented \citep{stoll1987program, kumar1992futures, cushing2000stock, hillion2004price,
comerton2011measuring}. Exchanges have responded by settling against averages over a window,
which raises the cost of moving the price because the mover has to trade throughout the
window. The National Stock Exchange of India settles stock futures and stock options at the
volume-weighted average price of the last thirty minutes of the expiry session. Whether this
window is nevertheless used to move the settlement price, a practice market participants call
settlement punching, is the question of this paper.

Two features of the setting make the question answerable. First, the exchange's order-level
files record every order and trade with the category of the participant, custodian,
proprietary or other client, and with the identity of the order on each side of every trade.
The direction of each participant category's trading in the window can therefore be measured,
and the limit order book reconstructed. Second, the settlement window exists on every
session, since the settlement price is simply the closing price, but it settles stock
derivatives on only twelve of them. The window on a monthly expiry can therefore be compared
with the window on every other session of the year, including sessions that share the
calendar features of an expiry without its settlement: the weekly expiries of the index
options, and month ends. A group of securities without derivatives, matched on turnover and
price, is exposed to the same calendar but to no settlement.

The analysis is organised around the distinction between activity and distortion. Expiry is
expected to raise activity in the window whether or not anyone attempts to move the price:
expiring positions are closed, rolled or hedged, and arbitrage positions are unwound at the
settlement price. Distortion is different. A settlement price moved by trading that does not
reflect information should be displaced from the prices before and after it, and should be
reversed once the trading stops. The tests therefore ask whether the settlement price is
reversed the next morning, whether this happens more on monthly expiries than on other
sessions, and whether it happens only in securities with an expiring contract.

The results separate the two cleanly. On monthly expiries the share of the session's volume
traded in the window rises by \ActWindowshareDidMonthlyexpiry{} percentage points more in
securities with derivatives than in the placebo group, and it does not rise on weekly index
expiries. The settlement price also moves further over the window on monthly expiries and is
partly reversed the next morning. But the reversal is equally large in the placebo group, and
the difference between the two is $\RevDidDriftxmonthlyexpiry{}$, with a randomization
$p$-value of \RevDidDriftxmonthlyexpiryRip{}. Aggressive flow by any participant category moves
the settlement price no more on expiry days than on other days, and is not reversed more. The
extra activity in the window is not accompanied by a distortion specific to the settlement.

The derivatives explain why. In 2022 stock derivatives were settled by delivery, so the
settlement price of an expiring future divides the payment for the delivered shares between
the final variation margin and the delivery price without changing its total. The expiring
future is priced accordingly: two minutes before the close, when nearly all of the settlement
price is determined, it still moves by \ConvExpiryMTwoEight{} basis points for each basis point
by which the cash price moves. For
futures holders the settlement price is an accounting price. It retains economic content for
stock options, whose exercise it decides, but on expiry days the settlement price lies no closer to
option strikes than on other days.

On the subset of sessions for which the full order book is reconstructed, the window is more
expensive to trade in on expiry days: spreads are wider, displayed depth is lower and a given
order moves the price further. The measures that separate directional pressure from heavier
two-sided trading, the variance ratio \citep{lo1988stock}, the persistence of signed order
flow and the permanent share of price impact, show no sign of sustained pressure.

The paper contributes to the literature on expiration-day effects \citep{stoll1987program,
stoll1991expiration}, including the evidence for India \citep{vipul2005futures,
agarwalla2013expiration}, which has documented abnormal volume and volatility around expiry
but has not been able to separate the settlement from the calendar or to observe who trades.
It applies the reversal test of the closing-price manipulation literature
\citep{carhart2002leaning, hillion2004price, comerton2011measuring} to a settlement window with
a placebo group and calendar controls, and it shows that the settlement method of the
derivative, delivery rather than cash, is central to whether the averaging window creates an
incentive to move the price at all. It also adds to the evidence on strike pinning
\citep{ni2005stock, avellaneda2003pinning, golez2012pinning} in a market where options are
settled by delivery.
