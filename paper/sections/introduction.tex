\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Derivatives that settle against a price formed in the underlying market give their holders an
interest in that price. When the settlement price is a single closing print, a participant with
a large expiring position can move it at little cost, and the resulting distortions are well
documented \citep{stoll1987program, kumar1992futures, cushing2000stock, hillion2004price,
comerton2011measuring}. Exchanges have responded by settling against averages over a window,
which raises the cost of moving the price because the mover has to trade throughout the window.
The National Stock Exchange of India sets the closing price of every security, and with it the
settlement price of its derivatives, as the volume-weighted average price of the last thirty
minutes of trading. Whether this window is nevertheless used to move the settlement price, a
practice market participants call settlement punching, is the question of this paper.

Two features of the setting make the question answerable. First, the exchange's order-level
files record every order and trade with the category of the participant, and identify the
buyer- and seller-initiated side of every trade, so the direction of order flow in the window
can be measured and the limit order book reconstructed. Second, the settlement window exists
on every session, but derivatives settle on only some of them. Stock derivatives settle on the
twelve monthly expiries; index options settle in cash on every Thursday. The window on these
sessions can be compared with the window on every other session of the year, in securities
with derivatives and in a matched placebo group without them, and for the Nifty 50 index
against the Nifty Next 50 index, which has no derivatives.

We test four hypotheses. Expiry concentrates trading in the window (H1). Trading in the window
is one-sided on expiry days, as measured by order imbalance (H2). The settlement price is
displaced by that trading, which would show as a reversal after the window or as a directional
move specific to what settles (H3). And the derivatives create an incentive to move the
settlement price, which depends on how they are settled (H4).

The answer depends on how the derivative is settled. Stock derivatives were settled by delivery
in 2022, so the settlement price of an expiring stock future divides the payment for the
delivered shares between the final margin and the delivery price without changing its total.
The expiring future is priced accordingly: two minutes before the close, when nearly all of the
settlement price is determined, it still moves by \ConvExpiryMTwoEight{} basis points for each
basis point of movement in the cash price. For stock derivatives the settlement price is
largely an accounting price, and the stock settlement shows no displacement specific to
securities with derivatives: its partial reversal on monthly expiries is equally large in the
placebo group.

The index is different. Index derivatives are settled in cash against the index's closing
value, and here the settlement is displaced. On the \IdxExpiries{} index expiries of the year,
the Nifty 50 moves over the settlement window by $\IdxSpreadDriftExpiry{}$ basis points
relative to the Nifty Next 50, compared with other sessions, with a randomization $p$-value of
\IdxSpreadDriftExpiryRip{}. The move takes place within the window, occurs on weekly expiries
as much as on monthly ones, appears in the index's constituents and not in stocks outside the
index, and is followed by a recovery the next morning of similar size, though that recovery is
imprecisely estimated. The index settlement value is systematically lowered on the days it
determines the payoff of the market's most traded derivative.

Expiry also changes the window in ways that do not depend on the settlement method. On monthly
expiries the share of the session's volume traded in the window rises by
\ActWindowshareDidMonthlyexpiry{} percentage points more in securities with derivatives than in
the placebo group, spreads widen and fleeting orders become more common, and order imbalance
persists more from one part of the window to the next. The measures that distinguish
directional pressure from heavier two-sided trading in the stock order books show no sustained
push.

The paper contributes to the literature on expiration-day effects \citep{stoll1987program,
stoll1991expiration}, including the evidence for India \citep{vipul2005futures,
agarwalla2013expiration}, which has documented abnormal volume and volatility around expiry but
has not been able to separate the settlement from the calendar or to observe the direction of
order flow. It applies the tests of the closing-price manipulation literature
\citep{carhart2002leaning, hillion2004price, comerton2011measuring} with a placebo group, a
placebo index and calendar controls, and shows that the settlement method, delivery or cash,
determines whether the averaging window is displaced. It also adds to the evidence on strike
pinning \citep{ni2005stock, avellaneda2003pinning, golez2012pinning}: neither stock nor index
settlement prices are drawn toward strikes.

Section~\ref{sec:background} describes the settlement arrangements and
Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses} states the hypotheses. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the

Sections~\ref{sec:activity} to~\ref{sec:derivatives} test the four hypotheses in turn, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
