\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Equity derivatives on the National Stock Exchange of India settle against the closing price of
the underlying security, and the closing price is the volume-weighted average price (VWAP) of
the last thirty minutes of trading. The settlement window therefore has a direct financial
consequence for every expiring position, and it is often argued that this invites trading
designed to move the settlement price, a practice market participants call settlement punching.
Whether it happens, and what the window looks like on expiry days if it does not, is the
question of this paper.

We ask four questions: how the cash VWAP evolves through the window relative to the traded
price of the stock future; which participants drive expiry-day volume; what role algorithmic
trading plays; and whether there is abnormal order book pressure, such as bursts of
cancellations or aggressive immediate-or-cancel orders, ahead of the settlement. The
order-level files of the exchange make each of these measurable. They record every order entry,
modification and cancellation with the category of the participant, custodian, proprietary or
other client, and whether it was submitted algorithmically; they identify the buyer and seller
of every trade; and they allow the limit order book to be reconstructed second by second.

These questions are organised as four broad hypotheses, each with one primary measure, and
the false discovery rate is controlled across the four primary tests. The settlement price behaves differently on expiry days (BH1);
trading in the window is more costly (BH2); trading in the window is more one-sided (BH3); and
order behaviour and participation change (BH4). The primary measures are, respectively, the
distance of the settlement VWAP from the mid at the start of the window, the quoted spread, the absolute order
imbalance and the share of orders cancelled within a second.

Each expiry is compared with a control session in the same month, for \DesignSymbols{} securities
in three groups: the most and least traded securities with stock derivatives, and a placebo group
of securities without derivatives, matched on turnover and price. The placebo group separates an
effect of the settlement from an effect of the calendar. The trade-based measures are also
computed for every session of 2022, with weekly index expiries and month ends as calendar
controls, and the derivatives files available for four of the expiries give the prices of the
expiring futures and options.

Two of the four primary tests are rejected, and both point to the same conclusion. The window is
more costly to trade in on expiry days: the quoted spread widens from \SpreadMeanBpsControl{} to
\SpreadMeanBpsExpiry{} basis points (BH2). And more orders are withdrawn within a second of entry,
\LifePhantomRateExpiry{}\% against \LifePhantomRateControl{}\% (BH4). Both changes are specific to
securities with derivatives. The other two are not rejected. The settlement VWAP ends no further
from the price at the start of the window (BH1), and trading in the window is not more one-sided
(BH3): the absolute order imbalance is unchanged, and the measures that distinguish directional
pressure from heavier two-sided trading point to congestion. The window on expiry days is
crowded, not pushed.

The supporting evidence explains why. The expiring stock future, which in 2022 was settled by
delivery, is priced on the cash price until the end of the window rather than on the settlement
VWAP, which leaves its holder with no gain from moving the settlement price. Where the settlement
is in cash the picture differs. On the \IdxExpiries{} index expiries of the year, the Nifty 50,
whose derivatives are cash-settled, falls over the window by about \IdxSpreadDriftPoints{} index
points relative to the Nifty Next 50, which has no derivatives (randomization
$p = \IdxSpreadDriftExpiryRip{}$). The settlement window is displaced where its price is paid out
in cash, and not where it only divides a delivery payment.

The paper contributes to the literature on expiration-day effects \citep{stoll1987program,
stoll1991expiration}, including the evidence for India \citep{vipul2005futures,
agarwalla2013expiration}, and to the study of closing-price manipulation \citep{carhart2002leaning,
hillion2004price, comerton2011measuring}, with order-level data that identify participants,
a placebo group and calendar controls.
